How to Import Spices From Indonesia: Documents, Shipping and Costs

Importing spices from Indonesia is straightforward once you understand the process — but first-time buyers routinely lose money on avoidable mistakes: incomplete documentation, the wrong Incoterm, or a container that arrives with moisture damage.

This guide walks through the full sequence, from first enquiry to delivered cargo.

Step 1: Define Your Specification Before You Ask for a Price

A quotation is only meaningful against a specification. Before contacting suppliers, decide:

  • Product and species — write the botanical name (for example Amomum compactum, not just "cardamom")
  • Grade — and what that grade means in measurable terms
  • Quantity — per shipment and per year, if you intend a recurring contract
  • Destination port — this determines freight and therefore the CIF price
  • Packaging — bag size, palletised or loose, any private label requirement

Suppliers who receive a vague enquiry will quote a vague price, and the gap will be filled at your expense later.

Step 2: Understand the Incoterms

The Incoterm determines where the seller's responsibility ends and yours begins. Three are commonly used in the spice trade:

TermSeller coversBuyer covers
FOB — Free On BoardGoods loaded onto vessel at Indonesian port, export clearanceOcean freight, insurance, import duties, delivery
CFR — Cost and FreightAs FOB plus ocean freight to destination portInsurance, import duties, delivery
CIF — Cost, Insurance and FreightAs CFR plus marine insuranceImport duties, customs clearance, inland delivery

New importers often default to CIF because it appears simpler. That is reasonable for a first shipment. Once you have established freight relationships, FOB usually gives you better control and a lower landed cost.

Step 3: Documents You Will Receive

A properly executed shipment from Indonesia includes the following. Missing any one of these can hold your cargo at destination customs:

Commercial Documents

  • Commercial Invoice — value, terms, and parties
  • Packing List — bag count, net and gross weight, container and seal numbers
  • Bill of Lading — the transport document and title to the goods

Regulatory Documents

  • Certificate of Origin (COO) — confirms Indonesian origin. Depending on your country, a preferential form (such as Form D for ASEAN, or Form E for China) may reduce your import duty. Ask for the correct form before shipment; it cannot always be issued retroactively.
  • Phytosanitary Certificate — issued by the Indonesian Agricultural Quarantine Agency, confirming the consignment is free from quarantine pests.
  • Fumigation Certificate — required by some destinations, particularly Australia and New Zealand
  • Certificate of Analysis — laboratory results against your agreed specification

Step 4: Check Your Own Country's Requirements

This is where first-time importers most often get caught. Import requirements differ substantially by destination:

  • European Union — requires compliance with maximum levels for aflatoxins and pesticide residues; consignments are subject to border control checks
  • United States — FDA prior notice is required before arrival, and the facility may need to be registered
  • Middle East — several markets require halal certification and legalised documents
  • Australia and New Zealand — strict biosecurity, commonly requiring fumigation treatment

Confirm these with your customs broker before placing the order, not after the vessel departs. Testing requirements in particular can add both cost and lead time.

Step 5: Container Planning

Most spice shipments move as full container load (FCL). A key point that surprises new buyers: spices are low density. A 20-foot container will usually reach its volume limit well before its weight limit.

  • Your freight cost per tonne is higher than a dense commodity like grain
  • Bag size and stuffing method meaningfully affect how much you can load
  • Requesting a container loading photo report is standard practice and worth doing

For moisture-sensitive cargo, ask whether the supplier uses desiccant bags and container liners. Ocean transit through the tropics produces significant condensation, and cargo that shipped within specification can arrive out of specification without it.

Step 6: Payment Terms

  • T/T with deposit — commonly 30% advance, 70% against copy of Bill of Lading. Typical for established relationships.
  • Letter of Credit (L/C) — more secure for both parties on larger first orders, but adds bank cost and requires precise document compliance.
  • Full advance — reasonable only for small trial orders or samples.

For a first transaction with a new supplier, an L/C or a modest trial order is the prudent path. Be cautious of any supplier who insists on 100% advance payment for a full container.

Step 7: Always Take a Sample First

A pre-shipment sample costs very little relative to a container. Approve it in writing against your specification, and retain a sealed portion so that any later dispute can be assessed against an agreed reference.

Realistic Timeline

StageTypical duration
Enquiry to quotation1–3 days
Sample dispatch and approval1–2 weeks
Order preparation and processing2–4 weeks
Documentation and export clearance3–7 days
Ocean transit (varies widely by route)2–6 weeks

Availability is seasonal. Planning your order against the harvest calendar, rather than against your own stock cycle alone, will get you better pricing and more consistent quality.

Five Mistakes That Cost Buyers Money

  1. Vague specifications. If it is not written down and measurable, it is not enforceable.
  2. Requesting the wrong COO form. A missed preferential certificate can mean paying full duty unnecessarily.
  3. Ignoring moisture control in transit. Specification is measured at loading, but you receive the goods at discharge.
  4. Skipping the sample. The cheapest possible insurance against a container-scale problem.
  5. Choosing on price alone. The lowest quotation frequently reflects a lower grade than the one you specified.
